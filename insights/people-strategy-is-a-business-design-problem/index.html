People strategy

People Strategy Is a Business Design Problem

When people decisions sit outside the operating model, organisations end up treating symptoms instead of designing for performance.

Femi Orimoloye·Original editorial perspective·3 min read

A considered perspective on people and performance

Key takeaways

  • Start with the business outcome, not the HR activity.
  • Diagnose work, structure and decision rights before prescribing a programme.
  • Connect measures to the intended change and revisit them after implementation.

People strategy is often treated as a specialist HR plan that follows the corporate strategy. The business sets its goals; the people function is then asked to recruit, train, reward and retain the workforce needed to deliver them. This sequence sounds sensible, but it can leave the most important questions unanswered: is the work designed well, are decision rights clear, and do the organisation’s systems make the desired performance possible?

The false separation

When people strategy is disconnected from business design, the organisation tends to respond to symptoms. A missed target triggers training; a slow process triggers recruitment; turnover triggers a retention initiative. Each intervention may be reasonable, yet none necessarily addresses the underlying cause. The problem could be conflicting priorities, unclear accountability, poor workflow, inadequate tools or a structure that separates decisions from the information needed to make them. Adding people to a badly designed system can increase its cost without improving its output.

A more integrated approach starts by translating the business strategy into the work and capabilities it requires. If the strategic promise is faster service, leaders must examine hand-offs, authority, staffing patterns, customer information and the incentives that shape responsiveness. If the promise is innovation, they must consider how teams share knowledge, test ideas and make decisions. People strategy becomes practical when it describes the capabilities and conditions needed for the business model to work, rather than listing HR programmes in isolation.

Start with outcomes and work

Begin with the outcome the organisation is trying to improve. Describe it in operational terms: quality, reliability, customer resolution, cycle time, growth, risk control or another meaningful result. Then map the work that produces it. Identify the critical activities, dependencies, decisions, exceptions and points where work waits or gets repeated. This creates a common fact base for business leaders, operations, finance and HR. It also prevents the conversation from jumping too quickly to a preferred solution such as hiring, restructuring or training.

Next, determine the capabilities that matter most. Capabilities are broader than job titles: they may include judgement, relationship management, data interpretation, technical execution, risk assessment or the ability to coordinate across functions. Ask where those capabilities sit today, how consistently they are applied, and which constraints stop them from producing the intended outcome. This analysis can reveal whether the organisation needs new skills, a different role design, better tools, clearer authority, stronger management routines—or a combination.

Design the operating system, not a single programme

Organisation design includes structure, but structure is only one part of the system. Decision rights determine who can act; processes determine how work moves; information determines what people can see; incentives shape what gets prioritised; and management routines determine how performance is reviewed. If these elements contradict one another, employees must work around the system. The informal workarounds may keep activity moving, but they often hide risk, increase dependency on individual knowledge and make performance difficult to reproduce.

Coherence matters more than the number of initiatives. Recruitment, development, performance management, reward and employee experience should reinforce a shared set of strategic priorities. For example, an organisation cannot credibly demand cross-functional collaboration while rewarding only local output. Nor can it expect sound judgement while requiring approval for every routine decision. Leaders should test the alignment between the behaviour they want and the conditions they have created for that behaviour to occur.

Use evidence without pretending it proves more than it does

People data can help diagnose a system, but metrics require context. High turnover in one team may indicate poor management, an unusually demanding workload, a changing labour market, a compensation issue or a deliberate shift in workforce composition. A correlation between an intervention and improved results does not, by itself, establish causation. Compare trends, segment the data carefully, examine operational context and seek qualitative evidence from the people closest to the work.

The Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development’s evidence review on people management emphasises the importance of strategically aligned practices and the need to consider how people practices operate as a system. Its discussion of the Ability–Motivation–Opportunity model is a useful reminder: capability alone is not enough if people lack motivation, resources or opportunity to contribute. See the [CIPD’s future-focused HR analysis](https://www.cipd.org/en/views-and-insights/thought-leadership/insight/people-professionals-maximise-impact/). This is evidence to inform design, not a universal formula guaranteeing a particular result.

A practical design sequence

A disciplined process can be run in stages. First, agree the business outcome and baseline. Second, map the work and its constraints. Third, identify the capabilities and decisions that determine performance. Fourth, diagnose the gaps between current and required conditions. Fifth, design a small number of interventions that address root causes. Sixth, assign owners, measures and review dates. This sequence is deliberately diagnostic: it delays solution selection until leaders understand what is actually preventing the desired result.

Make trade-offs explicit. Every design choice has consequences for cost, speed, control, flexibility, employee experience and risk. Centralising a decision may improve consistency but slow local response. Giving teams autonomy may improve speed but require stronger guardrails and capability. Outsourcing may provide access to expertise but create dependency or knowledge-transfer challenges. Good people strategy does not eliminate trade-offs; it makes them visible and ensures they are chosen in service of the business model.

Measure whether the design is working

Use a small chain of measures rather than one headline KPI. Track implementation, the behaviour or capability expected to change, and the business outcome that should follow. For example, a manager-development programme might measure participation, observed changes in feedback quality, team-level indicators and relevant operational outcomes. None alone tells the full story. The chain helps leaders see where the logic is working and where assumptions need revisiting.

Establish the baseline before launch and agree what would count as meaningful improvement. Where feasible, compare across teams, time periods or phased implementations; document other changes that could affect results. Review unintended consequences as well as benefits. A process may become faster but less accurate; a productivity target may improve while workload becomes unsustainable. The aim is better organisational performance, not simply better-looking metrics.

Applying the analysis in practice

A leadership working session should connect the strategic objective to the work, capabilities and operating conditions that make it possible. Bring the business owner, operations, finance and people leaders into the same discussion. Ask each function to state its assumptions, then identify where they conflict. The value of the session is not consensus for its own sake; it is a clearer set of choices and owners.

Test the design against an ordinary day and a difficult day. What happens when demand rises, a critical person is unavailable, a customer issue crosses teams or a risk requires escalation? These scenarios reveal whether the organisation can operate as designed or depends on informal workarounds. Document the gaps and decide which are tolerable, which require controls and which need redesign.

Keep a decision log. Record the problem, evidence, options considered, trade-offs, decision owner and review date. This makes the reasoning visible and helps the organisation learn when assumptions change. It also discourages repeated debates in which the same options are reopened because no one remembers why the earlier choice was made.

A useful diagnostic is to ask whether the same problem persists across different people and teams. If it does, investigate shared processes, incentives, technology and governance before assuming the issue is individual capability. If the problem is concentrated, examine local leadership, role clarity and workload. This distinction helps target the intervention and prevents a broad programme from being applied where a narrower fix is more appropriate.

When several interventions are required, sequence them. Clarify decision rights before training people to make decisions; repair the data before introducing a dashboard; align performance measures before asking teams to collaborate more closely. The order matters because one intervention may depend on another. Identify these dependencies in the plan and make them visible to the executive sponsor.

A common failure is to treat the design as a one-time reorganisation. Strategy, customer demand and technology evolve, so the operating model needs a review mechanism. Establish triggers that prompt reconsideration—for example, repeated service failures, changes in demand, material new risk or a sustained shift in required capability. A trigger does not automatically mean restructure; it means leaders should revisit the assumptions and evidence before the current design becomes a constraint.

Keep employees informed about the reasoning and practical consequences. People need to know how responsibilities, decision paths and success measures will change, what support will be available and where questions should go. Communication cannot remove every concern, but it can reduce avoidable ambiguity. Involve managers early enough that they can explain the change accurately and raise operational problems before they become embedded.

A useful test is whether the organisation can explain the link between strategic priorities and the capabilities it chooses to build. If the link is vague, leaders should revisit the diagnosis before adding more initiatives. A focused plan with clear trade-offs and measurable assumptions is generally more useful than a broad catalogue of desirable activities.

A decision-led diagnostic for executives

People strategy should be judged by whether it changes the conditions under which results are produced. This distinction matters because organisations can become very active without becoming more effective. New programmes, policies, dashboards and training sessions may all be well intentioned, yet their combined effect depends on whether they address the actual constraint in the work. Leaders should therefore resist beginning with the intervention they already know how to purchase or deliver. Begin instead with a clearly framed decision: what must improve, for whom, over what period, and what evidence would indicate that the current approach is not working?

A useful diagnostic compares three layers. The first is the intended outcome: the customer, operational or workforce result the organisation is trying to achieve. The second is the operating mechanism: the work, decisions, capabilities, resources and relationships that are expected to produce that result. The third is the observed experience: what employees and managers actually encounter, including delays, exceptions, workarounds and competing demands. The gap between these layers is often more informative than a single performance score.

For example, imagine a service team missing response-time commitments. It would be premature to conclude that the team simply needs more people. The organisation should test whether demand is forecast accurately, cases are routed to the right capability, staff have authority to resolve common issues, knowledge is accessible, and performance measures reward resolution rather than speed alone. The example is illustrative, not a claim about a specific employer. Its purpose is to show how a symptom can have several possible causes—and why diagnosis should precede investment.

A practical way to make the strategy-to-work connection visible

Translate the diagnosis into a small number of explicit design choices. State the problem in operational language, identify the people affected, and describe the mechanism through which the proposed change is expected to work. Record the assumptions behind that mechanism. If the intervention depends on managers having time to coach, for instance, the plan must address workload and priorities; a manager-training course cannot create time that the operating model does not provide.

  1. Establish a baseline. Agree on definitions and capture the current level of performance, experience, cost, quality or risk. Note where the data is incomplete or unreliable.
  2. Identify the constraint. Use process observation, employee input, operational data and manager judgement together. Treat each source as partial evidence rather than assuming one source explains the whole system.
  3. Choose a proportionate intervention. Consider process redesign, clearer decision rights, role changes, capability development, technology, incentives or staffing. Explain why the chosen option is more plausible than alternatives.
  4. Run a bounded test where feasible. Define the population, duration, expected signal and safeguards. A pilot can reveal implementation issues, but it does not automatically prove that an intervention caused the outcome.
  5. Assign ownership and review. Name the decision owner, the people responsible for delivery, the review date and the conditions under which the organisation will adapt, scale or stop the intervention.

This discipline is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It reduces the risk of solving the wrong problem, makes trade-offs visible and helps leaders learn when conditions change. It also makes the work of HR, operations, finance and technology more coherent: each function can contribute its expertise to a shared problem statement instead of optimising its own activity in isolation.

What to measure—and what not to assume

Use a balanced set of measures. A leading indicator may show whether a necessary practice is happening; a lagging indicator shows whether the desired result is appearing. Pair operational measures with relevant employee or customer experience evidence. Check whether apparent improvement in one measure creates a cost elsewhere—for example, faster case closure accompanied by more repeat contacts, or lower turnover accompanied by declining internal mobility.

Interpret results in context. Differences between teams may reflect workload, customer mix, role complexity, local labour markets or management changes rather than the intervention being evaluated. Where possible, compare like with like, document changes that occurred during the measurement period, and avoid claiming causation from a simple before-and-after comparison. Qualitative evidence can help explain a pattern, but anecdotes should not be presented as proof of prevalence.

CIPD’s evidence review of HR management finds that people practices and workplace outcomes must be assessed with attention to research quality and context. Use the original source to understand its scope, methodology and limitations before applying a finding to a different organisation, geography or workforce. External research can inform a decision; it cannot replace local diagnosis. The strongest professional judgement combines credible published evidence with transparent assumptions and the organisation’s own operational facts.

The executive question is therefore not merely, “Did we deliver the programme?” It is, “Did the change improve the condition we intended to improve, for the people and business outcomes that matter, and what evidence supports that conclusion?” That question moves the discussion from activity reporting to organisational learning.

A practical action plan

  • Translate one strategic priority into the work and capabilities required to deliver it.
  • Map the most consequential workflow, including decisions, hand-offs and recurring friction.
  • Check whether structure, incentives, tools and management routines reinforce the intended behaviours.
  • Define a baseline, an accountable owner and a review date for each major intervention.

Questions for leaders

  • What business outcome is this issue affecting in our organisation?
  • What evidence would distinguish a root cause from a symptom in the current situation?
  • Which decision or practice should change first, and who owns it?
  • How will we know whether the change helped, and what unintended effects should we watch for?

The point

People strategy is business design because it shapes the conditions under which work gets done. Its value is not measured by the number of HR programmes delivered, but by whether the organisation has the capabilities, operating conditions and management systems needed to achieve its objectives. Start with the work, design for the outcome, and use evidence to learn whether the system is improving.
