Employee experience

The Employee Experience Is Built Into the Processes

Culture is shaped not only by what leaders say, but by the ordinary systems through which people enter, work, grow and get support.

Femi Orimoloye·Original editorial perspective·3 min read

A considered perspective on people and performance

Key takeaways

  • Map important moments across the employee lifecycle.
  • Identify friction in policies, hand-offs, tools and decision-making.
  • Improve the process behind the experience, then measure the effect.

Organisations often describe employee experience through broad themes such as culture, engagement and belonging. Employees, however, encounter the organisation through practical moments: applying for a role, receiving a decision, accessing a tool, asking for help, resolving a conflict, being assessed or leaving the business. Each interaction teaches people what the organisation values and how much effort it takes to get work done.

The lived system

A process is an employee experience because it distributes time, information, discretion and dignity. A promotion process that is opaque creates uncertainty; a slow expense process shifts administrative burden onto employees; a performance conversation with no follow-up signals that development is not a priority. These experiences accumulate and shape trust.

Map the moments that matter across the employee lifecycle, but avoid designing a polished journey map that ignores operational reality. Observe where employees wait, repeat information, seek informal help or rely on a manager to interpret unclear rules.

For leaders, the implication is to make the underlying assumption visible and test it against the organisation’s actual context. The same practice can produce different results across roles, sectors and operating conditions. Reduce friction at the source

A common mistake is to treat every frustrating experience as a communication problem. Sometimes employees need clearer guidance. But confusion may also come from duplicated approvals, incompatible systems, contradictory policies or decision rights that are not defined. More communication can explain a broken process without repairing it.

For each high-friction moment, distinguish information problems from design problems. Simplify steps where possible, remove unnecessary hand-offs, make ownership visible and design for exceptions as well as the standard case. Involving users can reveal workarounds invisible to process designers.

Consistency with room for judgement

Consistent processes help people understand what to expect and reduce arbitrary treatment. Yet excessive standardisation can ignore legitimate differences in role, context or need. A good process defines the standard, the decision owner and the safeguards, while making clear when professional judgement is appropriate.

Managers are often the interface between formal policy and lived experience. They need guidance, authority and support to apply rules consistently. If managers are held responsible for outcomes but lack discretion or resources, the organisation creates frustration for employees and leaders alike.

Employee voice as diagnostic evidence

Listening tools can identify friction, but survey scores do not explain causes by themselves. A low score on workload, fairness or manager support should prompt investigation rather than a generic action plan. Combine quantitative signals with interviews, process data and direct observation where appropriate.

Close the loop by explaining what was heard, what will change, what cannot change immediately and why. Employees learn whether speaking up is worthwhile by observing the response. Repeatedly collecting feedback without visible action can create cynicism and reduce future participation.

Measure the experience and the work

Useful indicators may include time to complete a process, repeat contacts, unresolved requests, error rates, time to productivity, internal mobility, absence, turnover and employee-reported effort. Select measures that fit the moment being improved. A faster process is not automatically better if accuracy, fairness or accessibility declines.

Segment carefully and protect privacy. Different teams may experience the same process differently because of workload, manager practice or access to tools. Use data to locate patterns, then investigate before attributing blame. The goal is to improve the system, not label employees as the problem.

A practical experience redesign

Choose one high-impact employee moment. Map the current journey from the employee’s perspective and the operational owner’s perspective. Identify waiting, repetition, ambiguity and unnecessary effort. Agree a desired experience, redesign the process with users, pilot it and monitor employee and business outcomes.

Assign a named owner. Cross-functional processes often fail because everyone can identify the problem but no one has authority to resolve it. Ownership should include the ability to convene functions, make trade-offs and maintain the process after launch.

Applying the analysis in practice

Choose a specific moment rather than trying to redesign the entire employee lifecycle at once. A promotion decision, onboarding hand-off, equipment request or return-to-work process can be mapped with the people who use it. Record the steps, wait times, information requested more than once, unclear decisions and the points where employees need informal assistance to make progress.

Co-design a simpler future process with employees and operational owners. Define the minimum controls required, what information is genuinely necessary, who can decide and how exceptions are handled. Pilot the design with a representative group, then compare the old and new experiences. Include quality, accessibility and fairness in the evaluation, not just speed.

Publish a short response after implementation: what changed, what did not, what the pilot showed and who owns the next improvement. This demonstrates that experience work is an operational discipline with follow-through, rather than a temporary engagement campaign. It also gives employees a realistic basis for judging whether the organisation learns from their experience.

A process improvement should account for different employee circumstances. Frontline, remote, shift-based and office-based workers may face different access constraints. Employees with disabilities or different language needs may encounter barriers that are invisible in a standard journey. Include representative users and test accessibility rather than assuming that one successful pilot works equally well for everyone.

Decide how the process will be maintained after launch. Assign an owner to monitor recurring friction, review complaints and update guidance when policies or systems change. Without ownership, processes tend to accumulate exceptions and workarounds again. A periodic review should ask whether the intended experience still matches the way the work is actually performed.

The most meaningful experience measures are often close to the process itself. For onboarding, that might mean whether employees have the access, equipment and information needed to contribute; for internal mobility, it might mean how long decisions take and whether candidates receive clear feedback. Pair these indicators with employee perspectives so the organisation can distinguish a fast process from a good one.

Avoid treating employee experience as a separate layer of comfort added after productivity work is complete. Removing unnecessary friction can support both employee dignity and operational reliability. However, not every control is unnecessary, and not every preference can be met. Explain the purpose of essential steps and simplify the parts that do not add proportionate value.

Treat the redesign as a continuing service improvement. Collect feedback after the change, observe how the process works under real demand and make it easy to report new friction. The objective is not a perfect journey map; it is a process that remains clear, accessible and reliable as circumstances change.

Find the friction in the employee journey

Employee experience is shaped by the effort required to get work done, not only by formal programmes and engagement campaigns. This distinction matters because organisations can become very active without becoming more effective. New programmes, policies, dashboards and training sessions may all be well intentioned, yet their combined effect depends on whether they address the actual constraint in the work. Leaders should therefore resist beginning with the intervention they already know how to purchase or deliver. Begin instead with a clearly framed decision: what must improve, for whom, over what period, and what evidence would indicate that the current approach is not working?

A useful diagnostic compares three layers. The first is the intended outcome: the customer, operational or workforce result the organisation is trying to achieve. The second is the operating mechanism: the work, decisions, capabilities, resources and relationships that are expected to produce that result. The third is the observed experience: what employees and managers actually encounter, including delays, exceptions, workarounds and competing demands. The gap between these layers is often more informative than a single performance score.

For example, imagine a service team missing response-time commitments. It would be premature to conclude that the team simply needs more people. The organisation should test whether demand is forecast accurately, cases are routed to the right capability, staff have authority to resolve common issues, knowledge is accessible, and performance measures reward resolution rather than speed alone. The example is illustrative, not a claim about a specific employer. Its purpose is to show how a symptom can have several possible causes—and why diagnosis should precede investment.

A process-level experience audit

Translate the diagnosis into a small number of explicit design choices. State the problem in operational language, identify the people affected, and describe the mechanism through which the proposed change is expected to work. Record the assumptions behind that mechanism. If the intervention depends on managers having time to coach, for instance, the plan must address workload and priorities; a manager-training course cannot create time that the operating model does not provide.

  1. Establish a baseline. Agree on definitions and capture the current level of performance, experience, cost, quality or risk. Note where the data is incomplete or unreliable.
  2. Identify the constraint. Use process observation, employee input, operational data and manager judgement together. Treat each source as partial evidence rather than assuming one source explains the whole system.
  3. Choose a proportionate intervention. Consider process redesign, clearer decision rights, role changes, capability development, technology, incentives or staffing. Explain why the chosen option is more plausible than alternatives.
  4. Run a bounded test where feasible. Define the population, duration, expected signal and safeguards. A pilot can reveal implementation issues, but it does not automatically prove that an intervention caused the outcome.
  5. Assign ownership and review. Name the decision owner, the people responsible for delivery, the review date and the conditions under which the organisation will adapt, scale or stop the intervention.

This discipline is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It reduces the risk of solving the wrong problem, makes trade-offs visible and helps leaders learn when conditions change. It also makes the work of HR, operations, finance and technology more coherent: each function can contribute its expertise to a shared problem statement instead of optimising its own activity in isolation.

What to measure—and what not to assume

Use a balanced set of measures. A leading indicator may show whether a necessary practice is happening; a lagging indicator shows whether the desired result is appearing. Pair operational measures with relevant employee or customer experience evidence. Check whether apparent improvement in one measure creates a cost elsewhere—for example, faster case closure accompanied by more repeat contacts, or lower turnover accompanied by declining internal mobility.

Interpret results in context. Differences between teams may reflect workload, customer mix, role complexity, local labour markets or management changes rather than the intervention being evaluated. Where possible, compare like with like, document changes that occurred during the measurement period, and avoid claiming causation from a simple before-and-after comparison. Qualitative evidence can help explain a pattern, but anecdotes should not be presented as proof of prevalence.

CIPD’s evidence reviews provide a useful foundation for connecting people practices to workplace outcomes while avoiding simplistic cause-and-effect claims. Use the original source to understand its scope, methodology and limitations before applying a finding to a different organisation, geography or workforce. External research can inform a decision; it cannot replace local diagnosis. The strongest professional judgement combines credible published evidence with transparent assumptions and the organisation’s own operational facts.

The executive question is therefore not merely, “Did we deliver the programme?” It is, “Did the change improve the condition we intended to improve, for the people and business outcomes that matter, and what evidence supports that conclusion?” That question moves the discussion from activity reporting to organisational learning.

A practical action plan

  • Map one employee moment from beginning to end.
  • Separate communication problems from workflow and decision-right problems.
  • Pilot a redesign with the employees who use the process.
  • Track experience and operational quality together.

Questions for leaders

  • What business outcome is this issue affecting in our organisation?
  • What evidence would distinguish a root cause from a symptom in the current situation?
  • Which decision or practice should change first, and who owns it?
  • How will we know whether the change helped, and what unintended effects should we watch for?

The point

Employee experience is not produced by messaging alone. It emerges from the systems through which people do their jobs and interact with the organisation. Improving it means redesigning the work, decisions and support employees encounter—and checking whether the experience and the underlying operation have genuinely improved.
