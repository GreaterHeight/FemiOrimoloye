Total rewards

Total Rewards Is a System, Not a Payroll Line

Pay matters. So do fairness, clarity, benefits, recognition and the trust employees place in the decisions behind them.

Femi Orimoloye·Original editorial perspective·3 min read

A considered perspective on people and performance

Key takeaways

  • Look beyond base pay to the full experience of reward.
  • Check whether reward signals reinforce the behaviours the organisation needs.
  • Evaluate reward decisions against both employee value and organisational priorities.

Employees interpret rewards as a pattern of organisational choices. Base pay matters, but so do variable pay, benefits, recognition, career opportunity, manager behaviour and the credibility of decisions. A reward package can be financially competitive and still feel poor if it is confusing, inconsistent or difficult to access. Conversely, a coherent package communicates what the organisation values and helps employees understand the relationship between contribution, performance and reward.

Reward philosophy and business strategy

A reward philosophy should translate the organisation’s operating and talent priorities into explicit choices. A business competing on scarce expertise may emphasise skill premiums and development; a business that depends on consistent service may focus on reliable base pay, clear performance expectations and team-based recognition. These are not universal prescriptions. They are hypotheses to test against the roles, labour markets and behaviours that matter in a particular organisation.

Document the principles leaders will use to make trade-offs: market positioning, internal equity, affordability, performance differentiation, transparency and the balance between fixed and variable pay. A philosophy that promises to lead the market in every dimension is not a philosophy; it is an unfunded aspiration.

For leaders, the implication is to make the underlying assumption visible and test it against the organisation’s actual context. The same practice can produce different results across roles, sectors and operating conditions. Define the decision, identify what evidence would change it, and record the limits of the analysis so that a useful framework does not become a rigid rule.

Total value, not just salary

Employees experience total rewards over time. Benefits that are theoretically generous but difficult to understand or claim may have limited practical value. A bonus formula that employees cannot explain can weaken motivation even when payouts are meaningful. Recognition that is inconsistent across managers may feel arbitrary. Career opportunity can be a reward, but only when access to development and progression is credible.

Map the full employee value proposition: cash compensation, benefits, working conditions, flexibility, learning, career opportunity, recognition and the quality of day-to-day management. Ask different employee groups which elements they value and where friction exists. Avoid assuming that one package has equal meaning for every role, life stage or labour market.

Internal equity and external competitiveness

Market data helps organisations understand external competitiveness, but market matching is not the same as a fair or coherent pay system. Job scope, required skills, accountability, location and business context all matter. Market benchmarks can also be noisy when job matches are weak or survey samples are not comparable. Treat market data as an input to judgement rather than a substitute for it.

Internal equity requires consistent job architecture, defensible pay ranges and clear rules for movement within and between ranges. Leaders should be able to explain how role size, skill, performance and experience influence pay. Exceptions may sometimes be justified, but unmanaged exceptions become a parallel system that erodes trust and makes future decisions harder.

Affordability and sustainability

A reward decision creates a recurring commitment, not only a one-time expense. Model the total cost of salary increases, employer contributions, benefits, variable pay and likely workforce growth. Consider how costs behave under different business scenarios. A package that is attractive this year but impossible to sustain can force abrupt corrections later, damaging credibility.

Affordability should not become a blanket reason to avoid addressing inequity. Instead, use transparent prioritisation, scenario modelling and phased implementation where appropriate. Explain what can be changed now, what requires further analysis, and how progress will be reviewed. Silence encourages employees to fill information gaps with speculation.

Measure what rewards are achieving

Track more than total payroll. Depending on the organisation’s goals, useful measures may include offer acceptance, regretted turnover, pay-range position, benefit utilisation, internal mobility, pay-equity indicators, employee understanding and the relationship between variable pay and the outcomes it is meant to encourage. Interpret measures by role, level and location, protecting confidentiality in small groups.

Do not assume a reward change caused a change in retention or performance simply because the two moved together. Labour-market shifts, leadership changes and workload can also matter. Establish a baseline, state the theory behind the change and review both intended and unintended effects. Reward analytics should inform decisions, not create false precision.

A practical reward review

Begin with workforce and business priorities. Map the current reward elements and their costs. Test market positioning using defensible job matches. Review internal consistency and exceptions. Ask employees whether they understand the package and can access its benefits. Model alternatives. Then communicate the decisions, the rationale and the limits clearly.

Communication is part of the reward system. Employees need to understand total value, eligibility, performance conditions and how decisions are made. A well-designed package can be undermined by poor communication; a clear explanation cannot, however, compensate for an inequitable design. Both substance and explanation matter.

Applying the analysis in practice

A reward review should bring together HR, finance, business leaders and, where appropriate, legal or employee representatives. Establish a shared view of the current package, its recurring cost, the roles it is intended to support and the principles used to make decisions. Where data is incomplete, identify the limitation rather than presenting a benchmark as definitive.

Test the package from the employee’s perspective as well as the employer’s. Can employees explain the main elements? Do they understand eligibility and performance conditions? Can they access the benefits? Are managers applying rules consistently? These questions help separate a problem with the package itself from a problem with communication, administration or local practice.

Use scenario modelling before committing to a change. Compare the near-term cost with recurring obligations, likely workforce changes and the risk of creating new inequities. Define which outcomes should improve and what evidence would prompt a review. Reward decisions should be explainable, affordable and connected to the capabilities the organisation needs.

Separate policy intent from employee experience. A policy may be technically consistent while producing different outcomes because managers interpret it differently, information is unevenly distributed or employees have unequal access to opportunities. Review both the formal rule and its application. Where exceptions exist, examine whether they are justified, documented and reviewed rather than automatically assuming every exception is improper.

Communication should state what employees can expect and where discretion remains. Overpromising uniformity can be as damaging as opaque decision-making. Explain the principles, the process for raising a question and how decisions are reviewed. For sensitive equity work, communicate the method and aggregate progress without exposing individual compensation information or making unsupported conclusions about specific people.

Governance should identify who can approve pay exceptions, what evidence is required, how conflicts are reviewed and how patterns are monitored. Without this discipline, the reward philosophy can be bypassed one offer or one promotion at a time. The aim is not to eliminate all discretion; it is to make discretion visible, reasoned and consistent with the principles leaders have approved.

A review should also consider how reward interacts with performance management and career progression. If performance ratings are inconsistent, variable pay may amplify those inconsistencies. If progression criteria are unclear, employees may interpret pay differences as evidence of favouritism. Reward decisions are more defensible when adjacent talent processes are equally coherent.

The final review should make the rules understandable to decision-makers and employees. Document the rationale, the data used, the limits of the comparison and the next review date. This record supports consistency over time and helps leaders distinguish a deliberate exception from an undocumented precedent that has simply been repeated.

Test the total reward proposition

Reward choices communicate what the organisation values, not only what it can afford. This distinction matters because organisations can become very active without becoming more effective. New programmes, policies, dashboards and training sessions may all be well intentioned, yet their combined effect depends on whether they address the actual constraint in the work. Leaders should therefore resist beginning with the intervention they already know how to purchase or deliver. Begin instead with a clearly framed decision: what must improve, for whom, over what period, and what evidence would indicate that the current approach is not working?

A useful diagnostic compares three layers. The first is the intended outcome: the customer, operational or workforce result the organisation is trying to achieve. The second is the operating mechanism: the work, decisions, capabilities, resources and relationships that are expected to produce that result. The third is the observed experience: what employees and managers actually encounter, including delays, exceptions, workarounds and competing demands. The gap between these layers is often more informative than a single performance score.

For example, imagine a service team missing response-time commitments. It would be premature to conclude that the team simply needs more people. The organisation should test whether demand is forecast accurately, cases are routed to the right capability, staff have authority to resolve common issues, knowledge is accessible, and performance measures reward resolution rather than speed alone. The example is illustrative, not a claim about a specific employer. Its purpose is to show how a symptom can have several possible causes—and why diagnosis should precede investment.

A decision framework for reward investment

Translate the diagnosis into a small number of explicit design choices. State the problem in operational language, identify the people affected, and describe the mechanism through which the proposed change is expected to work. Record the assumptions behind that mechanism. If the intervention depends on managers having time to coach, for instance, the plan must address workload and priorities; a manager-training course cannot create time that the operating model does not provide.

  1. Establish a baseline. Agree on definitions and capture the current level of performance, experience, cost, quality or risk. Note where the data is incomplete or unreliable.
  2. Identify the constraint. Use process observation, employee input, operational data and manager judgement together. Treat each source as partial evidence rather than assuming one source explains the whole system.
  3. Choose a proportionate intervention. Consider process redesign, clearer decision rights, role changes, capability development, technology, incentives or staffing. Explain why the chosen option is more plausible than alternatives.
  4. Run a bounded test where feasible. Define the population, duration, expected signal and safeguards. A pilot can reveal implementation issues, but it does not automatically prove that an intervention caused the outcome.
  5. Assign ownership and review. Name the decision owner, the people responsible for delivery, the review date and the conditions under which the organisation will adapt, scale or stop the intervention.

This discipline is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It reduces the risk of solving the wrong problem, makes trade-offs visible and helps leaders learn when conditions change. It also makes the work of HR, operations, finance and technology more coherent: each function can contribute its expertise to a shared problem statement instead of optimising its own activity in isolation.

What to measure—and what not to assume

Use a balanced set of measures. A leading indicator may show whether a necessary practice is happening; a lagging indicator shows whether the desired result is appearing. Pair operational measures with relevant employee or customer experience evidence. Check whether apparent improvement in one measure creates a cost elsewhere—for example, faster case closure accompanied by more repeat contacts, or lower turnover accompanied by declining internal mobility.

Interpret results in context. Differences between teams may reflect workload, customer mix, role complexity, local labour markets or management changes rather than the intervention being evaluated. Where possible, compare like with like, document changes that occurred during the measurement period, and avoid claiming causation from a simple before-and-after comparison. Qualitative evidence can help explain a pattern, but anecdotes should not be presented as proof of prevalence.

Reward evidence should be interpreted by workforce segment, job context and labour market; a single average can conceal meaningful differences. Use the original source to understand its scope, methodology and limitations before applying a finding to a different organisation, geography or workforce. External research can inform a decision; it cannot replace local diagnosis. The strongest professional judgement combines credible published evidence with transparent assumptions and the organisation’s own operational facts.

The executive question is therefore not merely, “Did we deliver the programme?” It is, “Did the change improve the condition we intended to improve, for the people and business outcomes that matter, and what evidence supports that conclusion?” That question moves the discussion from activity reporting to organisational learning.

A practical action plan

  • Define the business question and the outcome that should change.
  • Gather relevant quantitative and qualitative evidence; document limitations.
  • Agree on a small number of actions, accountable owners and review dates.
  • Measure intended outcomes and unintended consequences, then adjust.

Questions for leaders

  • What business outcome is this issue affecting in our organisation?
  • What evidence would distinguish a root cause from a symptom in the current situation?
  • Which decision or practice should change first, and who owns it?
  • How will we know whether the change helped, and what unintended effects should we watch for?

The point

Total rewards is a system because employees experience the whole relationship, not isolated budget lines. The strongest approach connects business strategy, affordability, market evidence, internal fairness and employee understanding. Review the system as a portfolio of choices and measure whether it attracts, supports and retains the capabilities the organisation needs. The practical test is whether the organisation can explain the choice it made, show how the evidence informed it and learn from what happened next.
