People strategy

Culture Is What the System Rewards, Tolerates and Repeats

When people decisions sit outside the operating model, organisations end up treating symptoms instead of designing for performance.

Femi Orimoloye·Original editorial perspective·3 min read

A considered perspective on people and performance

Key takeaways

  • Start with the business outcome, not the HR activity.
  • Diagnose work, structure and decision rights before prescribing a programme.
  • Connect measures to the intended change and revisit them after implementation.

Values statements can express genuine aspirations, but employees infer the real culture from what happens when priorities conflict. Does a leader who delivers results through harmful behaviour face consequences? Does the organisation act on employee concerns? Are mistakes used for learning or concealed? These repeated experiences teach people what is safe, important and expected.

Distinguish stated and lived culture

Stated values describe the culture leaders want. Lived culture is revealed through decisions, habits, stories, resource allocation and the response to pressure. A gap between the two is not automatically proof of bad faith, but it is a practical signal that the organisation’s systems may not support its aspirations.

Use specific examples rather than abstract labels. “We value accountability” should translate into clear ownership, timely decisions and a fair response when commitments are missed. “We value inclusion” should be visible in access to information, opportunity, voice and consistent treatment. Observable behaviours make culture discussable and measurable.

For leaders, the implication is to make the underlying assumption visible and test it against the organisation’s actual context. The same practice can produce different results across roles, sectors and operating conditions. Define the decision, identify what evidence would change it, and record the limits of the analysis so that a useful framework does not become a rigid rule.

Examine incentives and consequences

People pay attention to what gets promoted, rewarded, funded and tolerated. If leaders praise collaboration but reward only individual outcomes, the incentive system sends mixed signals. If speaking up is encouraged in principle but punished in practice, employees learn to remain silent. Formal values are weakened by repeated contradictory consequences.

Review performance criteria, promotion decisions, reward, recognition, escalation practices and how leaders respond to failure. The purpose is not to remove accountability or all competition. It is to ensure the consequences of behaviour align with the culture the organisation says it wants and the outcomes it needs.

Understand the operating context

Culture is not uniform across every team. Different roles face different pressures, and local managers can shape the experience significantly. A company-wide survey may hide differences between functions, locations or shifts. Investigate patterns without assuming that every team shares the same norms.

Qualitative evidence helps explain what metrics cannot. Interviews, focus groups and observation can reveal informal rules, workarounds and stories employees use to understand how things really operate. Protect confidentiality and avoid treating anecdotal comments as proof of an organisation-wide pattern.

Make leadership behaviour consistent

Leaders reinforce culture through their attention and choices. What do they ask about in reviews? Which risks do they escalate? How do they handle disagreement? Do they model the standards they expect from others? Employees observe these choices more closely than they observe a values poster.

Culture change requires leadership consistency over time. A single campaign cannot compensate for decisions that repeatedly contradict the message. Leaders should acknowledge gaps, explain trade-offs and show how systems will change. Credibility grows when employees see a clear link between stated principles and actual decisions.

Turn values into operating practices

Translate a small number of values into behaviours, decision rules and management routines. For example, a value of learning might mean reviewing failures without concealment while still addressing negligence. A value of customer focus might mean giving teams authority to resolve defined issues and reviewing recurring causes of customer effort.

Embed these practices in hiring, onboarding, performance, reward, promotion, governance and change management. Avoid turning values into a long compliance checklist. The objective is to create consistent cues that help people make decisions when the policy does not specify every detail.

Measure progress without reducing culture to a score

Use employee voice, observed behaviours, decision patterns, misconduct and grievance data, internal mobility, customer outcomes and management practices as complementary signals. No single indicator represents culture in full. Track the areas most relevant to the organisation’s stated priorities and risk profile.

Be careful not to reward a better survey score while ignoring underlying issues. Psychological safety, for example, is not achieved by asking employees to report feeling safe; leaders must also examine how dissent, error and bad news are treated. Measures should trigger inquiry and action rather than become targets to manipulate.

Applying the analysis in practice

A leadership working session should connect the strategic objective to the work, capabilities and operating conditions that make it possible. Bring the business owner, operations, finance and people leaders into the same discussion. Ask each function to state its assumptions, then identify where they conflict. The value of the session is not consensus for its own sake; it is a clearer set of choices and owners.

Test the design against an ordinary day and a difficult day. What happens when demand rises, a critical person is unavailable, a customer issue crosses teams or a risk requires escalation? These scenarios reveal whether the organisation can operate as designed or depends on informal workarounds. Document the gaps and decide which are tolerable, which require controls and which need redesign.

Keep a decision log. Record the problem, evidence, options considered, trade-offs, decision owner and review date. This makes the reasoning visible and helps the organisation learn when assumptions change. It also discourages repeated debates in which the same options are reopened because no one remembers why the earlier choice was made.

A useful diagnostic is to ask whether the same problem persists across different people and teams. If it does, investigate shared processes, incentives, technology and governance before assuming the issue is individual capability. If the problem is concentrated, examine local leadership, role clarity and workload. This distinction helps target the intervention and prevents a broad programme from being applied where a narrower fix is more appropriate.

When several interventions are required, sequence them. Clarify decision rights before training people to make decisions; repair the data before introducing a dashboard; align performance measures before asking teams to collaborate more closely. The order matters because one intervention may depend on another. Identify these dependencies in the plan and make them visible to the executive sponsor.

A common failure is to treat the design as a one-time reorganisation. Strategy, customer demand and technology evolve, so the operating model needs a review mechanism. Establish triggers that prompt reconsideration—for example, repeated service failures, changes in demand, material new risk or a sustained shift in required capability. A trigger does not automatically mean restructure; it means leaders should revisit the assumptions and evidence before the current design becomes a constraint.

Keep employees informed about the reasoning and practical consequences. People need to know how responsibilities, decision paths and success measures will change, what support will be available and where questions should go. Communication cannot remove every concern, but it can reduce avoidable ambiguity. Involve managers early enough that they can explain the change accurately and raise operational problems before they become embedded.

A useful test is whether the organisation can explain the link between strategic priorities and the capabilities it chooses to build. If the link is vague, leaders should revisit the diagnosis before adding more initiatives. A focused plan with clear trade-offs and measurable assumptions is generally more useful than a broad catalogue of desirable activities.

Compare stated values with lived consequences

Culture becomes visible in the decisions people see, the behaviours that are rewarded, the breaches that are tolerated and the routines that are repeated. This distinction matters because organisations can become very active without becoming more effective. New programmes, policies, dashboards and training sessions may all be well intentioned, yet their combined effect depends on whether they address the actual constraint in the work. Leaders should therefore resist beginning with the intervention they already know how to purchase or deliver. Begin instead with a clearly framed decision: what must improve, for whom, over what period, and what evidence would indicate that the current approach is not working?

A useful diagnostic compares three layers. The first is the intended outcome: the customer, operational or workforce result the organisation is trying to achieve. The second is the operating mechanism: the work, decisions, capabilities, resources and relationships that are expected to produce that result. The third is the observed experience: what employees and managers actually encounter, including delays, exceptions, workarounds and competing demands. The gap between these layers is often more informative than a single performance score.

For example, imagine a service team missing response-time commitments. It would be premature to conclude that the team simply needs more people. The organisation should test whether demand is forecast accurately, cases are routed to the right capability, staff have authority to resolve common issues, knowledge is accessible, and performance measures reward resolution rather than speed alone. The example is illustrative, not a claim about a specific employer. Its purpose is to show how a symptom can have several possible causes—and why diagnosis should precede investment.

A culture diagnostic grounded in operating evidence

Translate the diagnosis into a small number of explicit design choices. State the problem in operational language, identify the people affected, and describe the mechanism through which the proposed change is expected to work. Record the assumptions behind that mechanism. If the intervention depends on managers having time to coach, for instance, the plan must address workload and priorities; a manager-training course cannot create time that the operating model does not provide.

  1. Establish a baseline. Agree on definitions and capture the current level of performance, experience, cost, quality or risk. Note where the data is incomplete or unreliable.
  2. Identify the constraint. Use process observation, employee input, operational data and manager judgement together. Treat each source as partial evidence rather than assuming one source explains the whole system.
  3. Choose a proportionate intervention. Consider process redesign, clearer decision rights, role changes, capability development, technology, incentives or staffing. Explain why the chosen option is more plausible than alternatives.
  4. Run a bounded test where feasible. Define the population, duration, expected signal and safeguards. A pilot can reveal implementation issues, but it does not automatically prove that an intervention caused the outcome.
  5. Assign ownership and review. Name the decision owner, the people responsible for delivery, the review date and the conditions under which the organisation will adapt, scale or stop the intervention.

This discipline is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It reduces the risk of solving the wrong problem, makes trade-offs visible and helps leaders learn when conditions change. It also makes the work of HR, operations, finance and technology more coherent: each function can contribute its expertise to a shared problem statement instead of optimising its own activity in isolation.

What to measure—and what not to assume

Use a balanced set of measures. A leading indicator may show whether a necessary practice is happening; a lagging indicator shows whether the desired result is appearing. Pair operational measures with relevant employee or customer experience evidence. Check whether apparent improvement in one measure creates a cost elsewhere—for example, faster case closure accompanied by more repeat contacts, or lower turnover accompanied by declining internal mobility.

Interpret results in context. Differences between teams may reflect workload, customer mix, role complexity, local labour markets or management changes rather than the intervention being evaluated. Where possible, compare like with like, document changes that occurred during the measurement period, and avoid claiming causation from a simple before-and-after comparison. Qualitative evidence can help explain a pattern, but anecdotes should not be presented as proof of prevalence.

Culture interventions should be evaluated through behaviour and operational evidence as well as employee perceptions. Use the original source to understand its scope, methodology and limitations before applying a finding to a different organisation, geography or workforce. External research can inform a decision; it cannot replace local diagnosis. The strongest professional judgement combines credible published evidence with transparent assumptions and the organisation’s own operational facts.

The executive question is therefore not merely, “Did we deliver the programme?” It is, “Did the change improve the condition we intended to improve, for the people and business outcomes that matter, and what evidence supports that conclusion?” That question moves the discussion from activity reporting to organisational learning.

A practical action plan

  • Define the business question and the outcome that should change.
  • Gather relevant quantitative and qualitative evidence; document limitations.
  • Agree on a small number of actions, accountable owners and review dates.
  • Measure intended outcomes and unintended consequences, then adjust.

Questions for leaders

  • What business outcome is this issue affecting in our organisation?
  • What evidence would distinguish a root cause from a symptom in the current situation?
  • Which decision or practice should change first, and who owns it?
  • How will we know whether the change helped, and what unintended effects should we watch for?

The point

Culture is produced through repeated organisational choices. Leaders shape it by what they reward, tolerate, fund, measure and model. To change culture, connect values to observable behaviours and redesign the systems that contradict them. Credibility comes from consistency between what the organisation says and what people experience. The practical test is whether the organisation can explain the choice it made, show how the evidence informed it and learn from what happened next.
