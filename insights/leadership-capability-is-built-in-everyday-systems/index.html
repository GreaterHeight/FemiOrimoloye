Leadership & organisational effectiveness

Leadership Capability Is Built in Everyday Systems

Leadership development is stronger when the organisation’s routines, incentives and decision rights reinforce the behaviours it expects from leaders.

Femi Orimoloye·Original editorial perspective·5 min read

Leadership & organisational effectivenessOriginal editorial draft

Development cannot sit apart from the job

Workshops and coaching can help leaders build insight, but capability is tested in the real conditions of leadership: setting priorities, allocating resources, resolving conflict, developing people and making decisions under uncertainty. If everyday systems reward behaviour that contradicts the development message, the system will usually win.

Make expectations observable

Terms such as strategic, collaborative or people-centred are too broad to guide action on their own. Translate them into observable practices: how leaders set goals, explain trade-offs, give feedback, run reviews, share information and respond when a plan fails. Specific expectations create a basis for coaching and for fairer, more consistent assessment.

Align the management routines

Performance reviews, talent discussions, succession planning, reward decisions and operating meetings all communicate what leadership means in practice. If the organisation says it values developing talent but never discusses internal mobility, or says collaboration matters while rewarding only local results, its routines send a mixed message. Aligning these mechanisms makes expectations credible.

Give leaders room and support to practise

Capability grows through application, feedback and reflection. Leaders need opportunities to practise new behaviours on meaningful work, support from their managers and clarity about the decisions they are authorised to make. Development should not become a substitute for fixing excessive workload, unclear authority or structural barriers that prevent good leadership.

Measure application, not attendance

Completion records show participation, not changed practice. A more useful review asks whether leaders apply the intended behaviours, whether teams experience a meaningful difference and whether the relevant organisational outcome is moving in the expected direction. The evidence may be qualitative or quantitative; it should be proportionate to the intervention and honest about limitations.

The organisational question

What in the current system makes the desired leadership behaviour easier, and what makes it harder? Answering that question shifts development from a catalogue of courses to the deliberate design of the environment in which leadership is practised every day.
