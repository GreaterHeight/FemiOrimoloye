Leadership & organisational effectiveness

Leadership Capability Is Built in Everyday Systems

Leadership development is stronger when the organisation’s routines, incentives and decision rights reinforce the behaviours it expects from leaders.

Femi Orimoloye·Original editorial perspective·3 min read

A considered perspective on people and performance

Key takeaways

  • Define the leadership behaviours the organisation needs.
  • Align routines, accountability and incentives with those behaviours.
  • Reinforce learning through practice, feedback and follow-up.

Leadership programmes can introduce useful concepts, but capability is tested in the real conditions leaders face: competing priorities, uncertain information, difficult conversations, resource constraints and pressure to deliver. If the organisation rewards short-term results at any cost, tolerates inconsistent behaviour or gives managers responsibility without authority, a workshop alone is unlikely to overcome those signals.

Define the leadership work

Begin with the decisions leaders must make and the outcomes they are accountable for. A leader in a highly regulated operation may need strong risk judgement and disciplined escalation; a leader in a growing service business may need to coordinate across functions and develop capacity. Generic competency lists become useful only when translated into observable behaviour in context.

Clarify what good leadership looks like at different levels. The expectations of a team supervisor differ from those of an executive responsible for enterprise trade-offs. Distinguish technical expertise from the ability to set direction, make decisions, coach people and create conditions for others to perform.

For leaders, the implication is to make the underlying assumption visible and test it against the organisation’s actual context. The same practice can produce different results across roles, sectors and operating conditions. Define the decision, identify what evidence would change it, and record the limits of the analysis so that a useful framework does not become a rigid rule.

Build capability through practice

Capability develops through application, feedback and reflection. Give leaders opportunities to practise real decisions, review outcomes and learn from mistakes without removing accountability. Stretch assignments, peer problem-solving, coaching and structured after-action reviews can connect learning to the work leaders actually perform.

Learning should be close enough to daily work that participants can test ideas soon after the programme. Managers need time and support to apply new practices; otherwise, urgent operational demands can displace development. Senior leaders should model the behaviours they expect rather than treating development as an obligation for others.

Align incentives and decision rights

A leader cannot consistently act on an expectation that conflicts with the incentive system. If collaboration is required but performance reviews reward only individual targets, collaboration will be fragile. If leaders are expected to develop talent but are penalised whenever a strong employee moves to another team, internal mobility will be discouraged.

Review the conditions surrounding leadership behaviour: workload, span of control, authority, information access, performance measures and consequences for raising risks. Some apparent capability gaps are actually system constraints. Development remains important, but it should not be used to assign personal blame for problems leaders cannot reasonably solve.

Make feedback specific and continuous

Feedback is more useful when it describes observable behaviour, its impact and a practical next step. Broad labels such as “not strategic enough” or “needs executive presence” are difficult to act on unless translated into decisions, communication or behaviours that can be observed.

Use multiple perspectives where appropriate, protect confidentiality and avoid treating any single assessment as a definitive measure of leadership potential. Follow feedback with a development plan, opportunities to practise and a later review of progress. The purpose is to improve performance, not to create a permanent label.

Create a system of accountability

Leadership accountability should connect outcomes, behaviour and the quality of the management process. Results matter, but leaders should also be accountable for how they achieve them, including safety, fairness, risk and the development of team capability. Otherwise, the organisation may celebrate results produced through practices that create future liabilities.

Establish regular reviews of talent, succession risk, team health and execution. Use these discussions to identify where leaders need support, where role expectations are unclear and where the operating model prevents good decisions. Succession planning should be a continuous view of capability and readiness, not a list created only before a vacancy.

Measure transfer into the work

Track participation as an implementation measure, not the final outcome. Look for changes in feedback quality, decision turnaround, delegation, internal mobility, team performance or other indicators relevant to the leadership challenge. Be cautious about attributing broad business changes to one programme when many factors influence results.

The WHO’s guidance on mental health at work includes manager training and organisational interventions among its recommendations for healthier workplaces: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240053052. This reinforces that leadership behaviour and work conditions are connected. Training can help, but it works best alongside clear systems and organisational support.

Applying the analysis in practice

Translate leadership expectations into observable routines. For example, a team may need clearer weekly priorities, faster escalation of risks, more specific feedback or better follow-through on commitments. Define what a person should do differently and where the behaviour can be observed. This makes development practical and reduces reliance on vague labels such as “more strategic” or “stronger presence.”

Create opportunities to practise the behaviour in live work, then review what happened. A manager can prepare for a difficult conversation, hold it, seek feedback and reflect on the outcome. A leadership team can run a decision review to test whether assumptions were challenged and accountability was clear. Learning becomes more durable when it is repeated in context.

Do not evaluate leaders without examining their operating conditions. If priorities change daily, spans are unmanageable or authority is unclear, the system may make consistent leadership difficult. Combine individual development with changes to workload, decision rights, incentives and support so that the expected behaviour is feasible as well as desirable.

A leadership intervention should be supported by the organisation’s calendar and priorities. If development is scheduled during peak operational demand without protected time, participation may be high while application remains low. Build practice and reflection into real management routines and make the expectation explicit to the leader’s own manager.

Use evidence proportionately. Qualitative feedback can show whether a behaviour is becoming visible; operational measures can show whether a relevant process is improving; employee outcomes can provide a further signal. None should be interpreted in isolation. Document other changes during the review period and be prepared to revise the original theory when the evidence does not support it.

Leadership systems also need fairness. Behavioural expectations should be applied consistently, and feedback should account for the context in which outcomes were achieved. A manager should not be rewarded for hitting a target by creating avoidable harm, nor automatically blamed for a result outside their control. Clear evidence, proportional judgement and a chance to respond make accountability more credible.

Review the system periodically with the leaders who use it. Ask which routines help them make better decisions, which requirements create duplicate work and where they lack information or authority. This review should not become a request to remove all challenge. It should improve the conditions that make high standards achievable and help the organisation identify emerging capability needs.

The leadership review should end with a small number of observable commitments, not an aspirational development list. Define what will be practised, where it will be observed, who will provide feedback and when progress will be reviewed. This makes improvement concrete while leaving room for leaders to adapt their approach to context.

Build leadership into the work

Leadership development is more likely to transfer when learning is reinforced by real work, feedback, authority and organisational incentives. This distinction matters because organisations can become very active without becoming more effective. New programmes, policies, dashboards and training sessions may all be well intentioned, yet their combined effect depends on whether they address the actual constraint in the work. Leaders should therefore resist beginning with the intervention they already know how to purchase or deliver. Begin instead with a clearly framed decision: what must improve, for whom, over what period, and what evidence would indicate that the current approach is not working?

A useful diagnostic compares three layers. The first is the intended outcome: the customer, operational or workforce result the organisation is trying to achieve. The second is the operating mechanism: the work, decisions, capabilities, resources and relationships that are expected to produce that result. The third is the observed experience: what employees and managers actually encounter, including delays, exceptions, workarounds and competing demands. The gap between these layers is often more informative than a single performance score.

For example, imagine a service team missing response-time commitments. It would be premature to conclude that the team simply needs more people. The organisation should test whether demand is forecast accurately, cases are routed to the right capability, staff have authority to resolve common issues, knowledge is accessible, and performance measures reward resolution rather than speed alone. The example is illustrative, not a claim about a specific employer. Its purpose is to show how a symptom can have several possible causes—and why diagnosis should precede investment.

A practice-based leadership development cycle

Translate the diagnosis into a small number of explicit design choices. State the problem in operational language, identify the people affected, and describe the mechanism through which the proposed change is expected to work. Record the assumptions behind that mechanism. If the intervention depends on managers having time to coach, for instance, the plan must address workload and priorities; a manager-training course cannot create time that the operating model does not provide.

  1. Establish a baseline. Agree on definitions and capture the current level of performance, experience, cost, quality or risk. Note where the data is incomplete or unreliable.
  2. Identify the constraint. Use process observation, employee input, operational data and manager judgement together. Treat each source as partial evidence rather than assuming one source explains the whole system.
  3. Choose a proportionate intervention. Consider process redesign, clearer decision rights, role changes, capability development, technology, incentives or staffing. Explain why the chosen option is more plausible than alternatives.
  4. Run a bounded test where feasible. Define the population, duration, expected signal and safeguards. A pilot can reveal implementation issues, but it does not automatically prove that an intervention caused the outcome.
  5. Assign ownership and review. Name the decision owner, the people responsible for delivery, the review date and the conditions under which the organisation will adapt, scale or stop the intervention.

This discipline is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It reduces the risk of solving the wrong problem, makes trade-offs visible and helps leaders learn when conditions change. It also makes the work of HR, operations, finance and technology more coherent: each function can contribute its expertise to a shared problem statement instead of optimising its own activity in isolation.

What to measure—and what not to assume

Use a balanced set of measures. A leading indicator may show whether a necessary practice is happening; a lagging indicator shows whether the desired result is appearing. Pair operational measures with relevant employee or customer experience evidence. Check whether apparent improvement in one measure creates a cost elsewhere—for example, faster case closure accompanied by more repeat contacts, or lower turnover accompanied by declining internal mobility.

Interpret results in context. Differences between teams may reflect workload, customer mix, role complexity, local labour markets or management changes rather than the intervention being evaluated. Where possible, compare like with like, document changes that occurred during the measurement period, and avoid claiming causation from a simple before-and-after comparison. Qualitative evidence can help explain a pattern, but anecdotes should not be presented as proof of prevalence.

CIPD’s evidence review on people managers highlights the importance of management skill and organisational support, not training alone. Use the original source to understand its scope, methodology and limitations before applying a finding to a different organisation, geography or workforce. External research can inform a decision; it cannot replace local diagnosis. The strongest professional judgement combines credible published evidence with transparent assumptions and the organisation’s own operational facts.

The executive question is therefore not merely, “Did we deliver the programme?” It is, “Did the change improve the condition we intended to improve, for the people and business outcomes that matter, and what evidence supports that conclusion?” That question moves the discussion from activity reporting to organisational learning.

A practical action plan

  • Define the business question and the outcome that should change.
  • Gather relevant quantitative and qualitative evidence; document limitations.
  • Agree on a small number of actions, accountable owners and review dates.
  • Measure intended outcomes and unintended consequences, then adjust.

Questions for leaders

  • What business outcome is this issue affecting in our organisation?
  • What evidence would distinguish a root cause from a symptom in the current situation?
  • Which decision or practice should change first, and who owns it?
  • How will we know whether the change helped, and what unintended effects should we watch for?

The point

Leadership capability is built in the repeated choices leaders make and the conditions that shape those choices. Define the work of leadership, create opportunities to practise, align incentives, provide useful feedback and measure behaviour in context. The organisation develops stronger leaders when it designs the system to make good leadership possible and expected. The practical test is whether the organisation can explain the choice it made, show how the evidence informed it and learn from what happened next.
